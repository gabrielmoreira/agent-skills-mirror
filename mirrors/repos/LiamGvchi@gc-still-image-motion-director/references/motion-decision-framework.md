# Motion Decision Framework

Use this reference to decide what kind of motion, if any, belongs to a still image.

## 1. Read the Image Before Naming an Effect

Identify five layers:

1. **Subject**: person, animal, plant, object, mechanism, landscape, symbol, specimen, or abstract form.
2. **State**: active, resting, suspended, balanced, compressed, archived, observed, weathered, or paused.
3. **Cause**: gravity, wind, water, tension, impact, rotation, heat, light, or intentional body movement.
4. **Structure**: focal region, axis, frame, crop, negative space, reading order, and visual boundary.
5. **Fragile elements**: text, numbers, grids, paper, thin lines, labels, diagrams, and cutout edges.

Do not choose motion until these layers are clear.

## 2. Motion Suitability

### Motion

Use one restrained but visible action when the subject already implies a direction or force.

Examples:

- a runner lowers their center of gravity;
- a swimmer advances slightly along a water line;
- a compass needle oscillates and settles;
- connected figures transmit tension through a rope;
- a reel rotates around its existing axle.

### Micro-motion

Use almost-still movement when the image is quiet, contemplative, archival, or compositionally fragile.

Examples:

- rain droplets descend inside a photographic window;
- a flower head shifts a few degrees in a light draft;
- water lines expand by a few pixels;
- a shadow or reflected light changes within a bounded photo;
- a resting figure shows one subtle breath.

### Static

Recommend no subject motion when movement contradicts the subject's meaning or would destroy the design.

Examples:

- a pinned specimen should not suddenly fly;
- a stone representing weight should not float;
- a document scan should not ripple like fabric;
- a paused mechanism may stay frozen when the title emphasizes stopped time;
- a sparse typographic composition may be stronger as a still.

If the user still needs a video file, use a minimal environmental trace, a bounded observation light, or a short hold. Keep the subject fixed.

## 3. Motion Selection Priority

Choose the first valid option:

1. **Intrinsic motion** — what the subject itself can physically do.
2. **Reaction motion** — what moves because of the subject: rope, water, cloth, shadow, foliage, or load.
3. **Observation motion** — light, focus, measurement, or scanning behavior that belongs to the image's concept.

Observation motion is a fallback, not a default aesthetic.

## 4. Amplitude

Use the lowest sufficient level:

- `0 — static`: no subject displacement.
- `1 — micro`: a few degrees, a few pixels, one breath, one small ripple, or one light pass.
- `2 — restrained`: one legible action that remains within the original region.
- `3 — expressive`: use only when explicitly requested or strongly supported by the composition.

For sparse posters and collages, default to level 0–1. For a clearly active body or mechanism, default to level 1–2.

## 5. Timing

For a default 4-second clip:

- begin with a short visual hold;
- perform one slow action;
- allow a small physical response;
- settle without looping unless the user asks for a loop.

Useful patterns:

- `hold → action → settle`;
- `tension builds → peak → slight release`;
- `left-to-right sequential activation`;
- `small oscillation → diminishing return → stop`;
- `environment changes while the subject remains fixed`.

Avoid constant high-amplitude motion. A quiet image usually needs pauses.

## 6. Composition-Specific Guidance

### Poster or editorial layout

Keep camera, paper, type, diagrams, and layout fixed. Animate only the bounded focal subject and one linked response.

### Collage with photo windows

Treat each photo boundary as a mask. Motion may occur inside a photo, but the photo frame, tape, colored blocks, and surrounding paper must stay fixed.

### Archive or specimen sheet

Respect the recorded or pinned state. Prefer observation, illumination, or a small environmental trace over subject animation.

### Mechanical diagram

Move only parts connected by visible joints or axes. Keep the mechanism continuous and preserve labels and dimensions.

### Abstract or geometric image

Follow an existing axis, orbit, sequence, or relationship. Do not add random particles or three-dimensional camera motion without evidence.

## 7. Batch Consistency

For a set of images, share only:

- duration range;
- camera policy;
- stability constraints;
- overall motion amplitude;
- ending behavior.

Do not share the actual subject action unless the images show the same subject and physical system. Consistency should come from restraint and rhythm, not identical effects.
