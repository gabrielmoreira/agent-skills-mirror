title: Reasoning behind requirements
---

A capable reader still lacks the subject model that makes an unfamiliar case decidable. A bare rule handles listed cases; the cause, distinction, and discriminator let the reader derive the same decision for a new one.

Facts may be stated directly. A choice, precaution, threshold, or order instead needs the reasoning that makes it preferable. Before expressing one as an imperative, make four things derivable:

1. **Failure:** the concrete harm or wrong result prevented, not the rule restated.
2. **Distinction:** the similar-looking cases that require different actions.
3. **Evidence:** the signal, authority, default, or threshold deciding which case this is, including who must settle an unavailable fact.
4. **Effect:** the resulting action, what it protects, and its completion state.

Derivable means the reader can infer each from what is stated, not that each gets its own sentence: one clause often carries several, and one the page already establishes needs no restating. One the reader cannot infer is written out, whatever it adds in length.

Words such as *only* and *do not* remain useful when they follow from that model. Without it, the first unlisted case is decided by prior belief.

A list that decides a branch, outcome, fallback, or allowed class asserts which cases the child agent will meet, and a list supplied by the requester or the material proves only its members, as [[principle:complete-enumeration]] says. Where a named authority fixes the members, keep that authority beside the decision. Otherwise find one in-scope case the outcome admits and the list does not name, and settle it: by a general rule, by narrowing the outcome so the case falls outside, or by an explicit gap. An example illustrates a decision and never closes a class.
