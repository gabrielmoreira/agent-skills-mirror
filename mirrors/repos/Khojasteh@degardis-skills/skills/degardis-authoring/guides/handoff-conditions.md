---
title: Hand-off conditions
applicability:
- When the work depends on a task's hand-offs
---

Take hand-off mechanics from the effective manual: how a hand-off differs from a cue, and how its applicability is read.

When a task's work reveals that another task's outcome is needed, before, after, or instead of finishing its own, that is a hand-off from the task that sees it, and the condition is that task's: it names the situation the work reveals, decidable there, rather than the target's cue or goal, since the target may be entered from other tasks under other conditions. Naming the sending task's situation is therefore a dependency in plain view. Omit the condition only where every finished state the task reaches includes the target's outcome. A condition that holds before the situation has arisen loads the target early, and one that keeps holding after the target's outcome is delivered repeats it; where two tasks hand off to each other because each can reopen the other's outcome, each condition must be one the other task's outcome can make false, or the route never ends.

State the condition once, in the hand-off's applicability; the task body's prose adds only what the condition cannot carry, such as what the target must receive.

## Items

One holding item is enough for the hand-off to apply, and conditions that must all hold share one item, so each item is one situation in the sending task's work that alone calls for the target, and the list holds each situation once. Decide what is one situation by what the sending task can check, not by how the prose divides.

Situations recognized from one observable fact are one: name that fact once, at the width of the need, rather than listing its instances as sibling items or as a series joined by *or* inside one, since the next instance would go unrecognized. Situations recognized from different evidence or at different points in the work are separate even where one sentence could join them: joined in one item, they hide that either suffices alone, make the reader parse which clause holds, and let an edit meant for one change the other. A repair whose defect is still unidentified before editing and a revision that has closed a review's findings after it, for example, arise at different points, so each takes its own item.

Test each item alone: suppose it is the only one that holds, and ask whether the target is then needed. An item that fails is part of another situation, such as a precondition or qualifier, rather than one of its own: join it with *and* to the item it qualifies, or delete it where it qualifies none; standing alone, it sends the work on wherever it holds. An item that needs a sibling's words to be read, through *also*, *otherwise*, or a pronoun pointing across, depends on that sibling the same way, so each item stands alone, with its own opener.

Before keeping two items, find for each a situation that satisfies it and not the other. Where neither has one, they are one situation worded twice, whether as paraphrases or as a series of synonyms, and the same holds for two clauses of one item: keep the wording that decides it most directly and delete the rest, since copies left in place drift apart until they name different situations. Where only one has such a situation, it is the wider and the other adds nothing: delete the narrower, unless the wider item is broader than the need, which makes the wider the one to narrow. The number of items follows from these tests and is never a target.

A long list that survives these tests is the usual sign that a task carries more than one responsibility. Ask what the target must deliver in each item's situation: where items call for different outcomes, the target is doing more than one task's work, or the sending task is, and the split belongs to the task that holds them, since a task names each target once and the hand-off cannot be split in its place.
